# [Interference Resource Allocation] Realization of a Threat Assessment and Interference Allocation System Based on Combinatorial Optimization [with MATLAB Code]

Introduction In modern warfare, the development of multi-interference aircraft on both air and naval platforms has significantly increased due to their widespread application based on principles of collaborative control to counter increasingly advanced radar systems. Threat Assessment and Interference Allocation (TEJA) as an intelligent collaborative combat mission, its automation is a hot research topic at present. Currently, the research on TEJA systems mainly focuses on allocation models and algorithms. The allocation model is primarily established based on performance indicators for electromagnetic attack effectiveness, and different models lead to different interference allocation decision outcomes and algorithm selections.
Many threat assessment and weapon allocation systems have recently been designed to distribute available launch units to enemy threats with certain threat levels [1-4]. These systems are useful for solving threat assessment and resource allocation problems, but they lack consideration of specific characteristics associated with the current electromagnetic spectrum environment in counter-analysis. Although some related scientific works have considered counter-analysis content [5-7], the main factor causing this limitation is the sensitivity of specific data in the study and the difficulty in obtaining data from field experiments.
Fortunately, some TEJA systems have been proposed to overcome this limitation by creating dynamic learning databases [8] and/or look-up tables [9-14]. These systems only address the threat of assigning interference resources, ignoring the specific selection of optimal interference techniques across different radar stages. In contrast, the system developed by Osner and Plessis is an exception, as it performs adversarial analysis in the following areas for TEJA systems: the impact of different interference techniques on each threat and radar stage, the constructive and destructive interactions between different interference techniques, and the future impact of current interference actions. Unfortunately, although the models proposed in [14] are functional and easy to operate, they still exhibit the following shortcomings in the decision-making process of TEJA: (1) there is no general method for calculating the probability of encountering radar parameters when not provided (such as radar range); (2) these analyses seem to overlook the fact that assigning multiple interference opportunities to a threat results in additive effects of interference on platforms under non-standard conditions; linearly; (3) using exhaustive search (EM) to test the TEJA model for finding the optimal gain, with increasing dimensionality of the JRA problem, the computational load significantly reduces the platform's survival probability; (4) there is still a lack of analysis of radar interaction.
From the perspective of multi-interference machine platforms, deficiencies (2) to (4) are key factors that weaken the level of collaborative operations. To overcome these shortcomings, our work proposes a TEJA (COTEJA) system based on combinatorial optimization. This system inherits the structure of the previous generation TEJA system [14], which includes a target function and a user-defined parameter list based on adversarial analysis. At the same time, COTEJA system also improves the performance of TEJA systems from multiple aspects, including the calculation formula for encounter probability, modification of the interference effect operator, interference encoding scheme reflecting collaborative interference strategy, and the extended permutation-based differential evolution (ePDE) algorithm for allocation. Additionally, the design of the look-up table can clearly explain the interaction between radars.

The problem statement is depicted in Figure 1, where the considered issue is to autonomously plan interference strategies for various threats using a computerized decision-making agent system on a platform. To address this issue, it is necessary to conduct adversarial analysis of the jamming-radar process within the electromagnetic spectrum domain, and then successfully model the TEJA system in conjunction with tactical situations.
The brief flowchart of the jamming-radar process is shown in Figure 2, which can roughly be divided into three levels at the system level. Considering the nature of the TEJA system as a high-level model, it is assumed that all radar feature parameters are captured by the lower-level system in an ideal environment.
Taking general self-defense jamming into account, the overall problem is that a cooperative UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle) formation equipped with multiple jammers (as a single air force offense/defense platform) enters enemy territory. The enemy territory consists of multiple radars, all equipped with ground-to-air weapons. This scenario initializes the positions of the jammer and threat radar using a three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system. It also considers other aspects of the threat, including the advanced level of the radar, radar distance, jamming distance, encounter probability, current radar phase, and progress of the radar phase.
In military environments, decision-making intelligent agents often need to rapidly allocate available jamming resources against enemy radar under high-pressure conditions. This mission must be completed within a certain time interval because the system updates real-time data from the electromagnetic environment to enhance its adaptability. To ensure the efficiency and accuracy of the TEJA system, the selection of algorithms and the setting of time intervals should achieve a balance. The red box in Figure 2 shows the flowchart of the TEJA system for each time interval.
This paper assumes that each jammer in the TEJA system is equipped with a Convolutional Modulation Signal Generator, which offers more effective interference effects than intelligent noise jammers based on digital radio frequency storage technology. It features simple structure, flexible control, and ease of expansion. The Convolutional Modulation Jamming utilizes multiple random pseudo targets to superimpose and cover target echoes in both time and frequency domains, significantly enhancing the interference effect on advanced radar systems and improving the adaptability of the jamming waveform. In the jammer, intercepted radar signals are sampled to produce coherent and quadrature digital signals, which are then transmitted to a pre-signal processor for complex convolution processing.
The convolution operation first performs multiple weighted delay and superposition based on distance cells on the input waveform to obtain noise and pseudo target data satisfying the required probability distribution and power spectral distribution. Subsequently, the sub-signal processor completes functions such as tap delay, amplitude weighting, and delay length control to cover all (or part) repetitive cycles of the radar signal in the time domain. By changing the weighting coefficients through software settings, five main jamming techniques can be achieved: noise jamming (NJ), coverage pulse jamming (CP), multi-pseudo target jamming (MFT), range-gated pullout jamming (RGPO), and velocity-gated pullout jamming (VGPO). It is evident that the control of jamming modes and adjustment of jamming parameters are highly flexible.
Furthermore, it is assumed that each radar mode can be divided into four stages in sequence: search, capture, tracking, and guidance. Each radar stage has a certain execution time. If the performance of the radar drops below a user-defined threshold during this period, the radar level is interrupted and reset to the search level [14].

The combination of different technologies can either enhance or weaken each other, as shown in the interaction between interference techniques in Table 2.

Since the principles of NJ and CP are similar, it can be assumed that they do not affect each other. NJ and MFT are the two most easily implemented coupling techniques in jammers, both significantly enhancing each other's interference effects, especially on search radars. Radar scan areas generate very dirty clutter bands with target completely submerged. The functions of CP and MFT are somewhat similar, aiming to deceive radar reconnaissance systems; CP can reduce the probability of the system detecting real targets.
Since the more clear the waveform of a decoy target, the easier it is for trackers to be interfered with, NJ has a side effect on RSNR and VSNR deception. Interestingly, we believe that CP has a positive impact on R and V. Firstly, CP blurs the position information of decoy targets but cannot cover them; secondly, after successfully breaking the lock on RND and VND, finding targets quickly becomes a significant challenge in the presence of CP interference. MFT weakens the interference effects of VPGO and RPGO because targets used to devise resistance strategies will be eliminated by random decoys. Finally, cooperation between VPGO and RPGO often strengthens each other, as the induced success rate of their combined technology will be greater than either individually.

Conclusion In light of the shortcomings of the previously proposed TEJA system, we have proposed a new combined optimization TEJA (COTEJA) system to overcome the limitations of the TEJA system. The COTEJA system modifies the objective function by supplementing the interaction between threat radars and selects appropriate combination optimization algorithms (including corresponding jamming encoding methods) to plan effective interference strategies. Due to the analysis of the jammer-radar combat process being fully considered in the system, during a complex mission design exercise, the system demonstrated good cooperative interference capabilities, validating the effectiveness of the joint noise-based deception and NJ. At the same time, the use of the ePDE algorithm allows complex optimization problems to be solved well within a certain time interval. The worst-case optimization still ensures a RUR of 97.35%. Importantly, the platform equipped with the COTEJA system survived after completing its mission.
In future work, there is an urgent need to develop an equipment configuration model that can adapt to various combat environments. There is a lack of sufficient prior knowledge and corresponding allocation algorithms.
